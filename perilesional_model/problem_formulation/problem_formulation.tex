\begin{grayb}\textbf{Purpose.} \newline
This note fully specifies the 3D time-harmonic MRE forward simulation
used to generate the shear-wave displacement field $u(\vect{x})$ inside
a water-balloon phantom, together with the two inversion pipelines
applied to $u$. Everything a code reviewer or a re-implementer needs
to reproduce the runs is here: geometry, boundary conditions, material
model, driver, discretisation, inversion recipes, and the numerical
scales that make the run well-posed.
\end{grayb}

\section{Physical picture}

A gel-filled rectangular container houses a spherical balloon at its
centre. The balloon has been inflated with water to \SI{250}{\milli\litre}
(radius $a = \SI{3.908}{\centi\metre}$) from a stress-free casting state
at \SI{50}{\milli\litre} (radius $A = \SI{2.285}{\centi\metre}$). A piston
plate at the bottom of the container is driven at \SI{60}{\hertz}; the
resulting shear-wave field is sampled inside the gel and inverted for
the local shear modulus. This mimics the MRE experiment reported by
Yin et al.\ (2026) as reproduced quantitatively by
\emph{paper\_lesion\_expansion.pdf}.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.80\textwidth]{schematic.png}
\caption{Container cross-section in the $y$--$z$ plane, showing the
five rigid walls (red \emph{u}=0), the traction-free top surface (blue
$\partial u/\partial z = 0$), the bottom piston plate (yellow), the
water-filled balloon (blue disk, no shear support), and the 12\,mm
perilesional shell used for ring statistics (black dashed circle).
The $x$-axis is into the page; the same BCs apply on the two out-of-page
faces (\SI{15}{\centi\metre} apart in $x$).}
\end{figure}

\section{Governing equation}

We solve the scalar Helmholtz equation for the complex tangential
displacement $u(\vect x) \in \mathbb{C}$ at fixed angular frequency
$\omega = 2\pi f$ with $f = \SI{60}{\hertz}$:

\begin{equation}
\rho\,\omega^2\, u(\vect x) \;=\; \nabla \cdot \bigl(G^*(\vect x)\,\nabla u(\vect x)\bigr)
\qquad \vect x \in \Omega,
\label{eq:helmholtz}
\end{equation}

where the complex-valued shear modulus incorporates hysteretic damping:

\begin{equation}
G^*(\vect x) \;=\; G(\vect x)\,(1 + i\,\xi),
\qquad \xi = 0.05
\quad(\Rightarrow\; Q\!\approx\!10).
\end{equation}

Physical constants:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
Symbol & Value & Meaning \\
\midrule
$\rho$ & \SI{1000}{\kilogram\per\metre\cubed} & gel density \\
$\omega$ & $2\pi\cdot 60$ \si{\radian\per\second} & angular frequency \\
$\xi$ & 0.05 & hysteretic damping ($Q\approx 10$) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Rationale for the scalar reduction: the acoustoelastic tangential
modulus $\mu_{\text{app},\theta\theta}$ appears in the scalar equation
governing the ring-tangential polarisation, which is what TSM MIP
isolates. Full vector Navier is available in a companion solver
(\texttt{vector\_elasticity\_3d.py}) but is not used here.

\section{Domain, grid and physical scales}

The container is Yin's \SI{15}{}\,$\times$\,\SI{15}{}\,$\times$\,\SI{18}{\centi\metre}
box. To fit the solver's cubic-grid constraint, we embed it in an
\SI{18}{\centi\metre} cube: the extra \SI{1.5}{\centi\metre} on each of
$\pm x, \pm y$ is filled with the same unstretched gel background, and
the container's rigid-wall BCs are applied at the cube faces
(the wall is thus \SI{1.5}{\centi\metre} outboard of the physical wall
location, a minor geometric approximation that does not affect the
perilesional region).

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
Symbol & Value & Meaning \\
\midrule
$N$          & 60           & voxels per axis \\
$\Delta x$   & \SI{3}{\milli\metre} & voxel edge \\
$L = N\Delta x$ & \SI{0.18}{\metre} & cube side \\
DOF          & $N^3 = \num{216000}$ & unknowns in the linear system \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Solver convention (matching \texttt{helmholtz\_fd\_3d.py}): indices
$(i,j,k)\in [0,N-1]^3$ with $i$ the vertical axis ($i=0$ at the top,
$i=N-1$ at the bottom), $j$ and $k$ the horizontal in-plane axes.

\paragraph{Shear-wave resolution.} At \SI{60}{\hertz} in unstretched gel:

\begin{equation}
c_s = \sqrt{\mu/\rho} = \sqrt{2750 / 1000} = \SI{1.66}{\metre\per\second}
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
\lambda_s = c_s / f \approx \SI{27.6}{\milli\metre}
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
\lambda_s / \Delta x \approx 9.2.
\end{equation}

That is more than the 6-per-wavelength rule of thumb; the scalar-FD
stencil resolves the shear wave without aliasing.

\section{Boundary conditions}

The container has D\textsubscript{4h}-broken BCs (five rigid walls,
one free top):

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
Face & Condition & Discrete implementation \\
\midrule
$i = 0$ \hfill (top)      & $\partial u/\partial z = 0$ & ghost mirror ($u_{-1}=u_{+1}$) \\
$i = N-1$ \hfill (bottom) & Dirichlet from driver       & set $u$ at all disk nodes \\
$j = 0$, $j = N-1$        & $u = 0$                     & Dirichlet \\
$k = 0$, $k = N-1$        & $u = 0$                     & Dirichlet \\
Balloon interior          & $G(\vect x) = \SI{1}{\pascal}$ & vanishing shear coefficient \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The "water-filled balloon that cannot support shear" is realised by
setting $G(\vect x) = \SI{1}{\pascal}$ inside the balloon. Equation
(\ref{eq:helmholtz}) then reduces to $\rho\omega^2 u \approx 0$ in the
interior, so $u \to 0$ there and the balloon acts as a shear-wave null
region. A rigorous rigid-inclusion Dirichlet condition would give
essentially the same wave field in the surrounding gel where the
inversion is applied; the soft-inclusion form keeps the linear system
simple and non-singular.

\section{Material field $G(\vect x)$}

\subsection{Kinematics: incompressible spherical cavity expansion}

The gel was cast around the balloon at the \SI{50}{\milli\litre} state, so
that state is stress-free ($\lambda_\theta \equiv 1$). Incompressibility
of the gel combined with spherical symmetry gives the exact isochoric
radial map from reference radius $R$ to current radius $r$:

\begin{equation}
r^3 = R^3 + \bigl(a^3 - A^3\bigr),
\quad
\lambda_\theta = \frac{r}{R} = \left(1 + \frac{a^3 - A^3}{R^3}\right)^{1/3},
\quad
\lambda_r = \lambda_\theta^{-2},
\label{eq:kinematics}
\end{equation}

with the current cavity radius set by injection volume,
$a = (3V/(4\pi))^{1/3}$. \textbf{This map is exact for the open-topped
container, not an approximation}: as argued in \S2 of the Sobh--Ehman
paper, in spherical symmetry incompressibility alone fixes the
stretch field, whatever the outer boundary does (the outer BC enters
only through the hydrostatic pressure). For $V = \SI{250}{\milli\litre}$,
$a = \SI{3.908}{\centi\metre}$; $\lambda_\theta$ at the cavity edge is 1.710
and at $r = a + \SI{12}{\milli\metre}$ is 1.159 (matches paper Table~1).

\subsection{Acoustoelastic (perilesional) modulus}

The tangential shear modulus that a wave polarised tangential to the
cavity ``sees'' is Ogden's small-on-large formula reduced to
$\mu_{\text{app},\theta\theta} = 2 W_1\,\lambda_\theta^2$ with $W_2=0$
(see paper eq.\ 4). For the two phantoms this evaluates to:

\begin{blueb}\textbf{Constitutive laws — mean-of-band material.} \newline
\textbf{P1} (plain \SI{10}{\percent} bovine gelatin, neo-Hookean matrix):
\begin{equation}
\mu_{\text{app},\theta\theta}^{\text{P1}} = \mu\,\lambda_\theta^{2}
\label{eq:p1}
\end{equation}

\textbf{P2} (\SI{8}{\percent} gelatin $+$ \SI{7}{\percent} cellulose fiber,
matrix + fiber; from paper eq.\ 8, verified symbolically):
\begin{equation}
\mu_{\text{app},\theta\theta}^{\text{P2}}
= \mu\,\lambda_\theta^{2}
+ \frac{k_1\,\lambda_\theta^{7}(\lambda_\theta-1)\exp\!\bigl(k_2(\lambda_\theta-1)^2\bigr)}
       {2\,(\lambda_\theta^{6} - 1)}
\label{eq:p2}
\end{equation}
\bigskip
\textbf{Mean-of-band material constants} (from paper Table~2):

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{llll}
\toprule
Symbol & Value & Units & Meaning \\
\midrule
$\mu$ & 2750 & \si{\pascal} & mean of $[2.5,3.0]$\,kPa (matrix, both phantoms) \\
$k_1$ & 3250 & \si{\pascal} & mean of $[1.5,5.0]$\,kPa (P2 fiber reinforcement) \\
$k_2$ & 1.25 & --- & mean of $[0.5,2.0]$ (P2 nonlinear stiffening) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\end{blueb}

Equation (\ref{eq:p2}) has the correct $k_1/12$ limit as
$\lambda_\theta \to 1$ (paper eq.\ 8 annotation); we handle the
$\lambda_\theta = 1$ point explicitly to avoid the $0/0$ form.

\subsection{$G(\vect x)$ maps}

Applying (\ref{eq:kinematics})--(\ref{eq:p2}) at every voxel produces
the ground-truth stiffness fields shown in Figure~\ref{fig:g}. The
ring is 2.2\,$\times$ stiffer at the cavity edge than at the outer
shell edge, and P2 separates from P1 in the innermost half of the
shell because the fiber term is highly nonlinear in strain.

\section{Driver model}

Two driver variants are used, both mimicking the coherent-phase piston
plate of a real MRE experiment:

\subsection{Bottom-plate driver (single-direction)}
Disk on the bottom face ($i = N-1$) of radius $r_d = 0.5 \cdot N \Delta x / 2 =
\SI{45}{\milli\metre}$, all disk nodes clamped to the same complex amplitude
$u_0 = \SI{1}{\micro\metre}$. 716 source nodes total. This is the
setup a laboratory MRE run uses.

\subsection{Multi-face broadband driver (for TSM MIP)}
Five faces driven simultaneously (bottom + four sides; top left free),
each with the same disk source ($r_d = \SI{45}{\milli\metre}$, amplitude
\SI{1}{\micro\metre}), 3580 source nodes total. Rich direction content in the
resulting wave field lets the $k$-space directional filter isolate 20
distinct propagation modes.

\section{Discretisation}

Second-order finite-difference discretisation of (\ref{eq:helmholtz})
on a regular cubic grid; the divergence form uses the harmonic mean
of $G^*$ at half-integer faces to handle sharp material gradients
correctly:

\begin{equation}
G_{i+\frac12,j,k}^{*}
= \frac{2\,G_{i,j,k}^{*}\,G_{i+1,j,k}^{*}}{G_{i,j,k}^{*} + G_{i+1,j,k}^{*}}
\quad\text{(similarly for $j,k$ faces).}
\end{equation}

Stencil at an interior voxel (7-point $+$ rho-mass diagonal):

\begin{equation}
\rho\omega^2 u_{i,j,k}
= \sum_{\text{faces }f} \frac{G_f^*}{\Delta x^2}\,(u_f - u_{i,j,k}),
\end{equation}

with $u_f$ the value at the neighbouring voxel across face $f$. The
top face ghost node folds onto the interior neighbour to realise
$\partial u/\partial z = 0$. Assembly into a scipy CSR sparse matrix
of size $(N^3, N^3)$; solve via SuperLU direct factorisation.

\begin{grayb}
\textbf{System size:} 216\,000 complex DOFs, $\approx 7$ non-zeros per row.
\textbf{SuperLU factorisation:} $\approx \SI{10}{\giga\byte}$ memory,
$\approx \SI{3}{\minute}$ wall time on a single core.
\end{grayb}

\section{Inversion pipelines applied to $u(\vect x)$}

\subsection{Direct inversion (DI)}

The locally-homogeneous approximation to (\ref{eq:helmholtz}):

\begin{equation}
G(\vect x) \;\approx\; -\rho\,\omega^2 \,\frac{u(\vect x)}{\nabla^2 u(\vect x)}
\end{equation}

evaluated with a 7-point centred Laplacian. A \SI{3}{}\,$\times$\,\SI{3}{}\,$\times$\,\SI{3}{}
spatial median filter is then applied to suppress inversion
artifacts at material gradients (matching Yin's own protocol). Real
part of the complex $G$ is retained.

\subsection{Yin TSM MIP (direction-filtered)}

Applied to the multi-face broadband run:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Generate 20 unit vectors $\ihat_d$ on a Fibonacci sphere.
\item For each direction, apply the Gaussian angular wedge filter in
$k$-space:
\begin{equation}
\hat u_d(\vect K) = \hat u(\vect K)\cdot
\exp\!\biggl(-\frac{1 - (\hat{\vect K}\cdot \ihat_d)^2}{\sigma_a^2}\biggr)
\cdot \mathbf{1}\!\bigl[\,|\vect K|/K_{\text{Nyq}} \in [0.02, 0.45]\bigr]
\end{equation}
with angular width $\sigma_a = 0.35$ rad ($\approx$ 20\textdegree\ FWHM).
\item Direct-invert each $u_d$ with 3\,$\times$\,3\,$\times$\,3 median.
\item Combine across directions:
\begin{align}
\mu_{\text{conv}}(\vect x) &= \frac{\sum_d |u_d(\vect x)|\,G_d(\vect x)}{\sum_d |u_d(\vect x)|}
&&\text{(amplitude-weighted mean)}\\
\mu_{\text{TSM}}(\vect x)  &= \max_{d}\ G_d(\vect x)
&&\text{(voxelwise MIP)}
\end{align}
\end{enumerate}

\section{Where the scalar formulation is an approximation}
\label{sec:scalar-approx}

Sections \ref{sec:scalar-approx}--\ref{sec:vector-navier} were added
after the initial write-up in response to two physics-check questions
from the review: \emph{should the stiffness tensor be transformed to
rectangular coordinates in order to solve for displacements?} and
\emph{should the stiffness be a matrix instead of a scalar?} The
answer to both is \emph{yes}, and this note now specifies how.

\subsection{Two coordinate frames}

The Sobh--Ehman acoustoelastic modulus $\mu_{\text{app},\theta\theta}$
is derived in the \textbf{spherical principal frame} of the balloon
expansion:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
Frame axis & Physical meaning \\
\midrule
$\hat r(\vect x)$ & radial (outward from balloon centre) \\
$\hat \theta(\vect x)$ & tangential (along the polar direction) \\
$\hat \phi(\vect x)$ & tangential (azimuthal) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

In that local frame the acoustoelastic tensor is diagonal
(Ogden's formula, incompressible cavity expansion with
$\lambda_\theta = \lambda_\phi$, $\lambda_r = \lambda_\theta^{-2}$):

\begin{equation}
\mu^{\text{sph}}_{ij}(\vect x) =
\operatorname{diag}\!\bigl(\mu_{rr},\, \mu_{\theta\theta},\, \mu_{\phi\phi}\bigr)
\label{eq:spherical-mu}
\end{equation}

with

\begin{equation}
\mu_{rr}(r) = 2 W_1(\lambda_\theta)\,\lambda_r^2
            = 2 W_1(\lambda_\theta)\,\lambda_\theta^{-4},
\quad
\mu_{\theta\theta}(r) = \mu_{\phi\phi}(r) = 2 W_1(\lambda_\theta)\,\lambda_\theta^{2}.
\end{equation}

The FD grid, on the other hand, is in \textbf{Cartesian coordinates}
$(x, y, z)$. To use (\ref{eq:spherical-mu}) inside a Cartesian
differential operator we must rotate it at every voxel:

\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;
\mu^{\text{cart}}_{ij}(\vect x)
= \mu_{rr}(r)\,\hat r_i(\vect x)\,\hat r_j(\vect x)
+ \mu_{\theta\theta}(r)\,\bigl(\delta_{ij} - \hat r_i(\vect x)\,\hat r_j(\vect x)\bigr)
\;}
\label{eq:rotation}
\end{equation}

where $\hat r(\vect x) = (\vect x - \vect x_{\text{cavity}})/r$ is the
Cartesian components of the local radial unit vector at position
$\vect x$. Equation (\ref{eq:rotation}) is the compact form of the
general rotation
$\mu^{\text{cart}}_{ij} = Q_{ik} \mu^{\text{sph}}_{kl} Q_{jl}$
that is valid when the two tangential eigenvalues are equal
(spherical symmetry of the load) — which they are for balloon inflation.

Every voxel in the gel carries a distinct rotation, so
$\mu^{\text{cart}}_{ij}$ is a \textbf{fully populated symmetric
$3 \times 3$ tensor field}: six independent components varying
spatially, not one scalar.

\subsection{Anisotropy magnitude}

At the cavity edge at 250\,\si{\milli\litre} ($\lambda_\theta = 1.71$):

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
Component & Formula & Value (P1) [\si{\kilo\pascal}] & Ratio to $\mu_{\theta\theta}$ \\
\midrule
$\mu_{\theta\theta}$ & $\mu \lambda_\theta^{2}$    & 8.04 & 1.00 \\
$\mu_{\phi\phi}$     & $\mu \lambda_\theta^{2}$    & 8.04 & 1.00 \\
$\mu_{rr}$           & $\mu \lambda_\theta^{-4}$   & 0.32 & 0.040 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{The ring is 25$\times$ stiffer in the tangential direction
than in the radial direction} — the acoustoelastic anisotropy is
large, not a small correction. A scalar $G(\vect x)$ representation
cannot capture that.

\subsection{Why the scalar version was defensible for the ring-recovery test}

Yin's TSM MIP explicitly isolates the tangential polarisation via a
20-direction $k$-space wedge filter. Both the propagation directions
it picks up (in the sphere tangent plane) and the polarisation it
measures are tangential, so the relevant modulus \emph{is}
$\mu_{\theta\theta}$. Using $G(\vect x) = \mu_{\theta\theta}(\vect x)$
in a scalar Helmholtz solve therefore recovers the correct
tangential-shear ring stiffness — which is what TSM sees.

The scalar-only sim misses three things a proper vector run captures:

\begin{itemize}
\item Wave polarisations that are not tangential — small in the ring
      itself but non-negligible near the cavity and container walls
      (they encounter the much softer $\mu_{rr}$)
\item Mode conversion at the balloon boundary, where a P-wave-like
      pressure fluctuation in the water couples into shear content in
      the surrounding gel
\item Wall reflections whose amplitude and phase depend on the
      anisotropic acoustic impedance mismatch
\end{itemize}

Second-order effects for a ring-mean comparison against Yin's numbers,
but genuinely absent from the scalar formulation. The vector Navier
upgrade in \S\ref{sec:vector-navier} folds them in.

\section{Vector Navier + rotated Cartesian tensor}
\label{sec:vector-navier}

The proper time-harmonic equation for a heterogeneous, anisotropic
solid is the Navier--Cauchy equation
\begin{equation}
\rho\,\omega^2\, u_i(\vect x)
= \partial_j \sigma_{ij}(\vect x)
\label{eq:navier}
\end{equation}
with constitutive law
\begin{equation}
\sigma_{ij} = \lambda\,\delta_{ij}\,\varepsilon_{kk}
              + \mu_{ik}(\vect x)\,\varepsilon_{kj}
              + \mu_{jk}(\vect x)\,\varepsilon_{ki},
\qquad
\varepsilon_{ij} = \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(\partial_i u_j + \partial_j u_i\bigr)
\label{eq:constitutive}
\end{equation}
where $\vect u(\vect x)$ is now a \textbf{vector} unknown and
$\mu_{ij}(\vect x)$ is the rank-2 tensor from
(\ref{eq:rotation}). $\lambda \gg \mu$ is a bulk-modulus penalty
enforcing near-incompressibility (we use $\lambda = 10^5\,\text{Pa}$).

This is the quasi-anisotropic form implemented in
\texttt{navier\_solve\_3d\_tensor\_mu}: the scalar isotropic case
$\mu_{ij} = \mu\,\delta_{ij}$ recovers the ordinary isotropic Navier
equation exactly, and the general symmetric rank-2 $\mu_{ij}$ captures
directional shear response.

\subsection{S-wave content from the vector field}

Since $\vect u$ now carries P-wave content as well as shear, the
proper analogue of the scalar wave field for a TSM inversion is the
\textbf{curl} (rotational part), which cancels the P-wave gradient
$\nabla \phi$:

\begin{equation}
\vect \omega(\vect x) = \nabla \times \vect u(\vect x)
\end{equation}

$\vect \omega$ is a complex vector field with three components. The
scalar we feed to direct inversion is $|\vect \omega|$. This is
implemented in \texttt{curl\_of\_displacement\_3d}.

\subsection{Upgrade path to the full acoustoelastic $C_{ijkl}$}

The quasi-anisotropic form (\ref{eq:constitutive}) with a rank-2
$\mu_{ij}$ captures the directional-shear response of the problem but
is \emph{not} the full acoustoelastic elasticity tensor. The complete
form is Murnaghan third-order:

\begin{equation}
C^{\text{eff}}_{ijkl}(\vect x) = C_{ijkl}^{0}
+ M_{ijklmn}\,\sigma_{mn}(\vect x)
\end{equation}
adding three more scalar constants $(l, m, n)$ to the two Lamé
constants. This is a 1--2 week extension of
\texttt{navier\_solve\_3d\_tensor\_mu} — the design is sketched in
that file's module docstring.
